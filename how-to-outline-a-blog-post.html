Research and outline a post before you write a word

The outline is where the thinking happens. Here is a full one, annotated, for a real post.

By TechConsulting EditorialPublished 13 November 2026Sources checked 13 November 202613 min read

The short answer: research first, then outline, then draft. Research means reading the primary sources for your central claims and recording each consequential claim with its evidence. If you cannot write that note for a heading, the heading is not ready, and drafting will not fix it.

Who this guide is for

This guide is for a blogger or small publisher who has an idea and wants to turn it into a post that holds together. You need a topic, access to the primary sources behind it, and about an hour for research and outlining before you start drafting. If you have not yet chosen an idea or a reader, start with the pillar guide, From idea to published post: the complete blogging workflow, which covers steps one and two. If you already have a draft, the editing guide, Draft with AI, then do a real editing pass, is the next step after this one.

The worked example: the same post as the pillar guide

This guide uses the same real post as the pillar guide: Google's October 2026 AI Content Guidance: What to Check Before Publishing, published 14 October 2026. The reader and the purpose sentence are set in step 2 of the pillar guide, From idea to published post: the complete blogging workflow. Everything in the outline below serves that sentence, or it was cut.

Step 1: Research the primary sources first

Start with the original source of the central fact, not with other people's summaries. For the example post, the central fact was a dated update, so the research started at Google's own pages. Read the guidance itself, then the update log, then the related policy pages. Take notes in your own words as you read, and copy the exact URL for each claim you might use. A link you cannot find again is not a source.

The primary sources for the example post were:

  • Google's generative AI content guidance, which explains how website owners should check AI-created material before publishing, and calls for manual fact-checking and review.
  • Google's search update log, which records the 1 October 2026 entry as a documentation update.
  • Google's helpful content guidance, which asks publishers to consider audience, original contribution, sourcing, and purpose, and warns against changing dates merely to make material appear fresh.
  • Google's thin affiliation policy, which describes affiliate content that reproduces merchant descriptions without original value, and recognises that affiliate pages can add value through comparisons, additional pricing information, and original reviews.

Secondary sources, such as other guides about writing blog posts, were used only to compare how the topic is usually explained. They were not used as evidence for a fact about Google's guidance. That distinction matters. If a claim about Google rests only on another blogger's summary, it is not checked, however confident the summary sounds.

Step 2: Keep a claim sheet, not a pile of notes

While you research, record each consequential claim in a small table before it disappears into polished prose. A consequential claim is one that changes what the reader should do, such as a date, a rule, a limit, or a recommendation. For the example post, the claim sheet looked like this:

Claim Evidence to check Editorial action
Google updated its AI content guidance on 1 October 2026 Google's search update log, 1 October 2026 entry State the date and call it a documentation update
The update is not a new ranking algorithm or a ban on AI writing Update log wording, and the guidance page itself Say what it is not, in the opening, to stop the common misreading
Publishers must manually check consequential claims before publishing Generative AI content guidance page Make manual checking a step in the process, not a passing mention
Titles, descriptions, and image alt text need review too Generative AI content guidance page Include metadata in the final reader check
Thin affiliate content adds no original value Thin affiliation policy Explain the policy, then give the recommendation to write the decision first
Changing dates to look fresh is warned against Helpful content guidance Include it in the after publishing advice

Notice what the claim sheet does not contain: opinions, adjectives, or background reading. Each row is a fact, a source, and a decision about how the post will handle it. If a claim has no source row, it does not go in the draft. That rule is what keeps a post honest when the draft starts to sound confident.

Step 3: Write the outline as headings plus a note each

An outline is not a list of topics. It is a list of headings in the order the reader will meet them, and each heading carries two notes: what evidence it needs, and what the reader should take from it. Write the headings first, then add the notes, then read the outline from top to bottom and ask whether a reader who only reads the headings would understand the argument. If the headings alone do not tell the story, the structure is wrong, and drafting will hide that instead of fixing it.

Keep the outline short enough to hold in your head. Ten to twelve sections is enough for a guide of this length. If your outline has twenty sections, you are probably writing two posts, or you have not decided what the post is for.

The full annotated outline of the example post

Below is the complete outline of the example post, Google's October 2026 AI Content Guidance: What to Check Before Publishing, with an annotation under each heading. The annotations explain why the section exists, what evidence it needed, and what it had to deliver to the reader. The headings and their order match the published post.

1. Opening: what the guidance is, and what it is not

Why this section is here: the reader arrives with a likely misunderstanding, that Google banned AI writing or changed its ranking. The opening has to correct that before anything else, or the rest of the post will be read through the wrong lens. Evidence needed: the update log entry dated 1 October 2026, and the guidance page itself. What the reader takes away: AI can help with research and writing, but the publisher is responsible for reviewing the work.

2. Who should pay attention?

Why this section is here: to name the reader and to define the terms a non-specialist needs, including SEO and affiliate article, in plain language. Evidence needed: none beyond the purpose sentence, because this section states the audience rather than making a factual claim. What the reader takes away: whether this post is for them, in one short section.

3. What changed, and what should you check?

Why this section is here: to give the reader the facts in a scannable table before the explanation begins, so a busy reader gets the essentials even if they read no further. Evidence needed: the guidance page, for facts, original contribution, titles and descriptions, and publishing at scale. What the reader takes away: four areas to check, and the warning that the October entry is a documentation update, not a paid service or certification.

4. What the guidance says

Why this section is here: to summarise the guidance accurately, including the point that quality rater assessments are distinct from direct ranking decisions. Evidence needed: the guidance page, read in full rather than summarised from another site. What the reader takes away: the editorial question that replaces "Does this sound human?", namely whether the page can support what it says and help its intended reader.

5. What will this process help you do?

Why this section is here: to state the benefit honestly and the cost honestly, in the same section, so the reader can decide whether the process is worth their time. Evidence needed: none, because this is a statement of what the process is for, not a factual claim. The cost is described as editorial time, and the section says plainly that no particular subscription is required. What the reader takes away: what the process catches, and what it costs.

6. Begin with a decision, not a word count

Why this section is here: to teach the purpose sentence method on a concrete case, a video tool comparison where the reader should be able to choose an option for producing twelve short clips a month. Evidence needed: none, because the section is method, illustrated with an example that is framed as a hypothetical choice, not a product claim. What the reader takes away: a sentence they can write before their own next post, and permission to remove material that does not serve it.

7. Keep a small claim sheet

Why this section is here: to give the reader the research tool itself, as a table they can copy, with rows for a free plan claim, a workload claim, a quality claim, and a user report. Evidence needed: the method, not a source, because the table is a template. The section also states the rule that changing information needs a verification date. What the reader takes away: a usable table and the rule that an undated source should not silently become today's price or limit.

8. Add the information a summary misses

Why this section is here: to show, rather than tell, the difference between weak copy and useful copy. Evidence needed: none, because both versions are labelled in the post as an invented example and a hypothetical version, with illustrative numbers, not the details of a real product. What the reader takes away: that useful writing explains an assumption and its consequence, and that friendlier adjectives are not an improvement.

9. Be precise about what you experienced

Why this section is here: to stop the reader from inventing a testing persona. It tells them how to describe a test they did, and how to label documentation review, expert observation, and community reports honestly. Evidence needed: none, because this is editorial method. What the reader takes away: the rule that evidence should be described, not converted into a stronger claim than it supports.

10. Affiliate content needs an independent contribution

Why this section is here: to answer the question an affiliate publisher actually has, whether their pages are at risk, with the policy itself rather than with reassurance. Evidence needed: the thin affiliation policy, linked directly. What the reader takes away: the recommendation to write the decision first, include drawbacks and free alternatives, and keep the recommendation understandable even if the affiliate link is removed.

11. Give the draft one final reader check

Why this section is here: to turn the whole post into a checklist the reader can run, so the post ends with an action rather than a summary. Evidence needed: the guidance page, for the inclusion of titles, descriptions, and alt text in the review. What the reader takes away: six questions to ask before publishing, plus the editing advice to remove repeated conclusions and vague praise.

12. What this checklist cannot promise

Why this section is here: to state the limit honestly, because a guide that promises rankings would be misleading. Evidence needed: none, because the section states what the process does not do. What the reader takes away: that completing a review does not guarantee rankings, traffic, or inclusion in an AI answer, and that an unresolved claim should be verified, qualified, or removed.

What was left out, and why

An outline is also a record of cuts. Three things were left out of the example post, and each cut was deliberate. A history of artificial intelligence was cut, because it does not help the reader run the check. A list of AI writing tools was cut, because the post is about the checking process, not about tool choice, and because no tool had been tested for the post. A prediction about future Google updates was cut, because there was no source for it. If a section cannot point to evidence or to the purpose sentence, it does not belong in the outline, however interesting it is.

Test your outline before you draft

Before you write a word of draft, read your outline aloud and check four things. First, do the headings alone tell the story in order? Second, does every heading have a note about its evidence, or is it resting on an unchecked claim? Third, is there a section that exists only because you like the topic, and not because the reader needs it? Fourth, does the outline end with an action, a decision, or a check, rather than trailing off? If any answer is no, fix the outline. Fixing an outline takes minutes. Fixing a draft that was built on a weak outline takes hours, and usually means rewriting.

What this costs, and where it falls short

Research and outlining cost time, and they slow down publishing compared with drafting straight from memory or from an AI summary. That is the trade. The cost of skipping them is higher: a post that is wrong, vague, or unsupported, and corrections that arrive from readers instead of from your own claim sheet. The main restriction is that an outline cannot create evidence. If the primary sources do not support the post you wanted to write, the outline will show that, and the honest response is to narrow the post or do more research, not to draft around the gap.
