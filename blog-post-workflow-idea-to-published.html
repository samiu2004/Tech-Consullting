From idea to published post: the complete blogging workflow

One post, carried through every step, so you can see what each step actually produces.

By TechConsulting EditorialPublished 13 November 2026Sources checked 13 November 202611 min read

The short answer: a blog post is written in eight steps. Find an idea a real reader needs, decide who the reader is and what they should be able to do after reading, research and record the claims you will need to support, outline before you draft, write a rough draft, edit it against your sources, run a publishing check, then watch how it performs and fix what is wrong. Most weak posts fail at step two or three, not at the writing itself. They start with no clear reader and no checked facts, so no amount of polishing can save them.

Who this guide is for

This guide is for a new blogger, a small business owner, or a creator who publishes on their own site and wants a repeatable process instead of starting from a blank page every time. You do not need an SEO background. SEO means improving how search engines can discover and understand a website. You do need a topic you can research honestly, access to the primary sources for your claims, and time to edit. If you are looking for tools to make videos or voiceovers, see the Content Creation hub instead. This guide is about writing, not about tool prices.

The worked example: one real post, all the way through

Most workflow guides describe the steps in the abstract. This one does not. Every step below shows what it looked like for one real post already published on this site: Google's October 2026 AI Content Guidance: What to Check Before Publishing, published 14 October 2026. That post is the example, and each step links to the cluster guide that covers it in depth where one exists. You can open the finished post at any point and check whether the step did what this guide says it should do.

The example post started from a single dated fact. Google updated its generative AI content guidance on 1 October 2026, adding information from its Search Quality Raters guidelines. The update log describes a documentation change. It does not announce a new ranking algorithm or a blanket ban on AI writing. That distinction, documentation change rather than new rule, became the spine of the whole post. If the writer had missed it, the post would have been wrong in its first paragraph.

Step 1: Find an idea a real reader needs

Start with a question someone is actually asking, not with a topic you feel like covering. Good sources for ideas are questions readers send you, search queries you can see in your own analytics, a dated change in an official source, or a decision a reader has to make this week. For the example post, the trigger was the dated Google update on 1 October 2026, and the reader question behind it was simple: publishers using AI wanted to know what they had to check before publishing, and whether the update changed the rules.

Test an idea with three questions before you commit to it. First, can you name the reader in one sentence? Second, can you find a primary source for the central claim? A primary source is the original publisher of the information, such as Google's own guidance pages, rather than a summary of it. Third, can you say what the reader will be able to do after reading? If any answer is vague, keep looking. A vague idea becomes a vague post.

What this step produced for the example post: the idea, stated in one line: "Google updated its AI content guidance on 1 October 2026, and publishers need a practical check they can run before publishing AI-assisted content."

Step 2: Decide the reader and the purpose, in writing

Before any research, write two sentences and keep them visible while you work. Sentence one names the reader. Sentence two finishes this prompt: "After reading this page, someone should be able to..." For the example post, the reader was anyone publishing website content with AI assistance: business owners, bloggers, editors, and people writing product comparisons. The purpose sentence was that the reader should be able to run a fact and usefulness check on an AI-assisted draft before publishing it.

This step matters because it decides what to leave out. The example post does not include a history of artificial intelligence, because that history does not help the reader run the check. It does include the thin affiliation policy, because that policy decides whether an affiliate article is useful or not. Every section has to earn its place by serving the purpose sentence. If a section does not, cut it, however interesting it is.

Step 3: Research, and record the claims before you draft

Research before drafting, and record each consequential claim with its evidence in a small claim sheet while you read. The outlining guide shows the full method and the example post's claim sheet: Research and outline a post before you write a word. Do not trust yourself to remember where a fact came from after the draft is written.

Use primary sources first. For the example post, those were Google's own pages: the generative AI content guidance, the search update log, the helpful content guidance, and the thin affiliation policy. Other guides are useful for seeing how a topic is usually explained, but they are not evidence for a fact. If only a secondary source supports a claim, say so, or leave the claim out.

Step 4: Outline before you write a word

An outline is a list of headings in order, each with a note about what evidence it needs and what the reader should take from it. Writing the outline first is what stops a post from wandering, and it is where most of the thinking happens.

The example post was outlined before it was drafted, and the full annotated outline is shown in the companion guide, Research and outline a post before you write a word. That guide shows every heading of the example post with a note explaining why the section exists and what evidence it needed. Read it if you want to see the outline itself. The point here is the order of work: outline first, draft second, never the other way round.

Step 5: Draft fast, and do not edit while you draft

Write the draft in one pass, following the outline, and resist the urge to fix sentences as you go. A draft is allowed to be rough. Its job is to get the structure and the claims onto the page. If you stop to polish the opening paragraph, you will run out of time before you reach the section that actually helps the reader.

If you use AI to help with the draft, treat its output as a rough draft, not as a finished post. AI can help with research and structuring, but the finished page still needs accurate information, checked sources, and something useful for its reader. That is the position Google's guidance takes, and it is the position this site takes too. The editing pass that turns an AI-assisted draft into a publishable post is covered in Draft with AI, then do a real editing pass, which shows a real before and after of the same draft, with every material edit explained.

What this step produced for the example post: a full draft that followed the outline, with the weak product copy example and the improved version both present, so the reader could see the difference between praise and useful detail rather than being told about it.

Step 6: Edit against your sources, not against your taste

Editing is not proofreading. Proofreading catches typos. Editing checks whether each claim is supported, whether the structure serves the purpose sentence, and whether vague praise is doing the work that evidence should do. The companion guide, Draft with AI, then do a real editing pass, runs the example draft through the three passes, facts, purpose, then language, with a before and after you can inspect.

The example post went through this kind of pass. Its invented product example, a fictional tool called ClipMaker, is labelled as invented in the post itself, and its numbers are labelled illustrative, not the details of a real product. That labelling is part of the edit. A reader should be able to tell what is evidence, what is a hypothetical example, and what is a reported experience.

Step 7: Run a publishing check before you press publish

Before publishing, review the page against its original purpose with a short checklist, the same kind of check the example post recommends to its own readers:

  • Does the opening answer the main question?
  • Are consequential claims sourced and current, with a verification date where the information changes?
  • Are tests, vendor claims, and reported experiences distinguishable from each other?
  • Is there an example, calculation, or explanation that adds useful substance?
  • Do the title and description accurately represent the article?
  • Can the reader take the next step without guessing?

Also check the parts readers see before the article: the title, the description, and the image alt text. Google's guidance explicitly includes those in the review. Then check the technical basics: the page has one clear title, the links work, the images have credits where required, and the page is listed where readers can find it. On this site, that means the hub page and the sitemap, not just the article itself.

Step 8: After publishing, watch and fix

Publishing is not the end of the workflow. A post about a dated guidance update needs a checked date, and it needs revisiting when the guidance changes. If a claim becomes outdated, verify it, qualify it, or remove it, rather than letting confident wording conceal the gap. Do not change dates merely to make material appear fresh. That is a specific warning in Google's helpful content guidance, and it applies to your own posts as much as to anyone else's.

Watch for the signals that tell you a step was skipped. Readers asking a question the post should have answered means step two was weak. Corrections from readers mean step three or six was weak. Readers leaving after the first paragraph usually means the opening did not answer the question, which is a step seven failure. Fix the step, not just the sentence.

What this workflow costs, and where it falls short

The main cost is editorial time: researching primary sources, keeping a claim sheet, outlining, and editing in separate passes. No particular subscription is required for this workflow, and paid tools may assist the work, but they do not replace the checking. The main restriction is that the workflow is slow compared with publishing AI output untouched. That is the point. A fast post that is wrong, vague, or unsupported costs more in corrections and lost trust than the time saved.

The workflow also cannot promise rankings, traffic, or inclusion in an AI answer. The example post says this about its own checklist, and it is true of this guide too. A good process improves a page for its reader. It does not guarantee how a search engine will treat it. If a claim is still unresolved after research, the honest options are to verify it, qualify it, or remove it.
