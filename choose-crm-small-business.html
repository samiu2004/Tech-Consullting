How to Choose a CRM for a Small Business

Start with the work your team needs to complete, then shortlist software against real requirements.

By TechConsulting EditorialPublished 15 November 20264 min read

Five CRMs can look almost identical in a feature table. Each promises a clearer pipeline, better follow-up and less administration. The useful differences appear when you ask a team to complete a real sales task.

Before booking another demo, write down what happens between a new enquiry and a completed sale. Choose software around that process. A long list of features is not evidence that your team needs them, and a familiar brand is not proof that it fits your budget.

Map the sale before choosing the system

Describe how a lead arrives, who owns it, what happens next and what makes it a won or lost opportunity. Include repeat purchases and the handoff to delivery if those matter to the business. A short, repeatable transaction needs different support from a proposal reviewed by several people over months.

That distinction is a starting point, not the entire decision. Both businesses may need reliable contact history, permissions and reporting. Write down which tasks must work on the first day, which would be convenient and which are unlikely to be used.

For a hypothetical three-person agency, the essential tasks might be assigning an enquiry, recording a proposal, reminding the owner to follow up and checking the expected revenue. This is a requirements example, not a tested customer setup.

Build a shortlist around hard requirements

Use the table below before comparing editions. Replace the sample answers with your own. “Required” should mean the team cannot reasonably complete its work without it.

Requirement Sample agency answer How to check it
Lead ownership Every open enquiry has an owner Create a test lead and assign it
Follow-up Owner sees the next action Set a reminder and check its delivery
Email history Relevant messages are visible to permitted staff Test visibility with two users
Reporting Owner can review outstanding proposals Build the actual report
Exit Contacts and important relationships are recoverable Export and inspect a sample

Check the minimum edition that meets each requirement. A feature listed on a vendor's homepage may belong to a higher plan or an add-on. Keep a link to the edition documentation beside the answer rather than relying on a sales slide.

Calculate the cost of a working setup

Count the people who need to edit records, send messages or manage the system. A view-only account, a sales seat and an administrator may not have the same price or permissions. Ask whether a required integration, onboarding service or usage allowance adds to the bill.

Write down first-year and ongoing costs separately. Include internal setup time, training, any overlap with your existing tool and the time needed to clean imported data. A low subscription can still be an expensive choice if the team must maintain several workarounds.

Use the HubSpot and Pipedrive comparison for a worked comparison structure, not as a substitute for your own requirements.

Test the work, not a polished demonstration

Use sample data you are allowed to share. Ask a likely daily user to create an enquiry, record a change, find the next action and complete a handoff. Watch where they need help. Test on the devices and connection they actually use.

Check an export before committing. Some exports cover selected records, not every attachment, activity or relationship in an account. The pre-purchase evaluation checklist explains how to test the exit without performing a full migration.

When a spreadsheet is enough

If one person handles a small number of opportunities and rarely misses a handoff, a shared spreadsheet with an owner, status and next-action date may meet the immediate need. Consider a CRM when repeated tasks, collaboration or reporting have become difficult to manage reliably.

Do not buy a larger system to compensate for an undefined process. Start with a short requirements sheet, test a qualifying edition and record the reasons for the decision. The right outcome can be a purchase, a smaller plan or staying with the existing process until the problem is clearer.
