Draft with AI, then do a real editing pass

A draft is not a post. Here is what a real editing pass changes, and why.

By TechConsulting EditorialPublished 13 November 2026Sources checked 13 November 20269 min read

The short answer: edit an AI-assisted draft in separate passes, one for facts, one for purpose, and one for language, and do not publish until each consequential claim has a source you have checked yourself. A draft that has only been proofread is still a draft.

Who this guide is for

This guide is for a blogger or small publisher who drafts with AI assistance and wants the finished post to be accurate and useful. You need a draft, the primary sources behind its central claims, and time to edit in passes rather than in one rushed read. If you do not have a draft yet, start with the pillar guide, From idea to published post: the complete blogging workflow. If you do not have an outline, read Research and outline a post before you write a word first, because editing cannot fix a post that was never structured.

The worked example: the same post, again

This guide uses the same real post as the other two guides in this cluster: Google's October 2026 AI Content Guidance: What to Check Before Publishing, published 14 October 2026. The reader and the purpose sentence are set in step 2 of the pillar guide, From idea to published post: the complete blogging workflow. The before and after below is a worked demonstration on this site, built from that topic and those sources, so you can see each edit and judge whether it was needed.

Pass 1: Facts first, before any language fixes

Read the draft once for facts only. Check every consequential claim against its source, and check the dates. For the example topic, the facts to check are the update date, 1 October 2026, the nature of the update, a documentation change rather than a new ranking algorithm or a ban, and the specific requirements, manual fact-checking and review, including titles, descriptions, and image alt text. If a claim has no source, you have three honest options: verify it, qualify it, or remove it. Rewriting it in smoother language is not a fourth option.

Keep a claim sheet while you edit, the same table described in the outlining guide. One row per claim, with the claim, the evidence, and the editorial action. If a sentence in the draft does not match a row, either the sentence is unsupported or the claim sheet is incomplete. Fix whichever one is wrong.

Pass 2: Purpose and structure

Read the draft a second time for purpose only. Does the opening answer the main question? Does each section help the reader do the thing in your purpose sentence? Is there a section that exists only because it sounds impressive? AI drafts often add background, history, and repeated conclusions that postpone the answer. Cut them. A long introduction that delays the answer is not thoroughness. It is a reader leaving.

Also check the labels. A reader should be able to tell what is evidence, what is a hypothetical example, and what is a reported experience. In the example post, the fictional product example is labelled as invented, and its numbers are labelled illustrative, not the details of a real product. That labelling is part of the edit, not decoration. If your draft presents a hypothetical example as if it were a real test, that is a factual error, not a style choice.

Pass 3: Language, last

Only now, with the facts and the structure settled, should you edit the language. Remove repeated conclusions, vague praise, and filler phrases such as "in today's fast paced world" or "revolutionary". Replace adjectives with specifics: instead of "powerful features", say what the feature does and what limit applies. Shorten sentences that hide their subject. Read the draft aloud if a sentence feels long, because your ear will catch what your eye skips.

Do not try to make the draft sound more human by adding personality. A post sounds human when it is specific, sourced, and honest about what it does not know. Adding casual phrases to a vague draft makes it a vague draft with casual phrases.

Before and after: the same draft paragraph, edited

The demonstration below is exactly what it says it is: a short draft paragraph written in a generic AI style for this article, about the example post topic, followed by the edited version of the same paragraph. It is not presented as output from a named tool, and it is not a transcript of a real drafting session. It is a worked demonstration, so that every change can be shown and explained. The facts in the edited version come from the primary sources linked in the outlining guide: Google's generative AI content guidance and Google's search update log.

Before: the draft paragraph

In today's rapidly evolving digital landscape, Google has unveiled groundbreaking new AI content rules that will completely transform how creators publish online. These powerful guidelines are essential reading for anyone who wants to stay ahead of the curve and unlock the full potential of AI powered content creation. With cutting edge best practices and revolutionary insights, this comprehensive guide will help you master AI content and achieve amazing results.

After: the edited paragraph

Google updated its generative AI content guidance on 1 October 2026, adding information from its Search Quality Raters guidelines. The update log describes a documentation change. It does not announce a new ranking algorithm or a blanket ban on AI writing. The guidance asks publishers to check AI-created material manually before publishing, and to review titles, descriptions, and image alt text alongside the article itself.

Every material edit, explained

What changed Why
Removed "In today's rapidly evolving digital landscape" Filler opening that says nothing and postpones the answer. The reader needs the date and the fact, not a scene setter.
Removed "groundbreaking new AI content rules" and "completely transform" Overstatement. The update was a documentation change, not a new set of rules, and the draft gave no evidence for a transformation.
Added the date, 1 October 2026, and the source of the added information, the Search Quality Raters guidelines These are the two checkable facts the paragraph exists to deliver. Without them, the paragraph contains no information.
Added "It does not announce a new ranking algorithm or a blanket ban on AI writing" Corrects the most likely misreading directly, using the update log wording, rather than letting the reader assume the worst.
Replaced "stay ahead of the curve" and "unlock the full potential" with the actual requirement, manual checking before publishing The original phrases are vague praise. The replacement tells the reader what to do.
Replaced "cutting edge best practices and revolutionary insights" with the specific review items, titles, descriptions, and image alt text Specifics replace adjectives. The reader can now act on the sentence instead of admiring it.
Removed "master AI content and achieve amazing results" An uncheckable promise. Editing cannot promise rankings, traffic, or results, and neither can the post.

Notice what the edit did not do. It did not make the paragraph longer for its own sake, and it did not add personality. It made the paragraph shorter in hype and longer in facts. That is the test for every edit in this guide: after the edit, can the reader do something they could not do before, or check something they could not check before? If the answer is no, the edit was cosmetic.

The final reader check

Before publishing, run the same final reader check the pillar guide sets out in step 7, From idea to published post: the complete blogging workflow: six questions about the opening, the sources, the evidence labels, the substance, the title and description, and the next step, plus a check of the title, description, and image alt text. Google's guidance explicitly includes those in the review. If any answer is no, fix the draft, not the checklist.

What editing costs, and what it cannot do

Editing in passes costs time, and it is slower than publishing a draft untouched. That is the trade, and it is the same trade described in the pillar guide. No particular subscription is required for this editing process. Paid tools may assist the work, such as a grammar checker or an AI writing assistant, but they do not replace the fact check, and this guide does not recommend a specific tool or claim a price for one. For tools in general, see the Content Creation hub.

Editing also has a hard limit: it cannot fix missing research. If the draft has no sources, no amount of language editing will make it trustworthy. It cannot turn a hypothetical example into evidence, and it cannot make an unresolved claim true. If a claim is still unresolved after research, verify it, qualify it, or remove it. Editing also cannot promise rankings, traffic, or inclusion in an AI answer. The example post says this about its own checklist, and it is true here too. A good edit improves a page for its reader. It does not guarantee how a search engine will treat it.
