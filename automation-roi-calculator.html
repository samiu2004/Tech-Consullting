Automation ROI Calculator: Cost, Capacity and Payback

Estimate first-year value and simple payback with your own assumptions, including setup and recurring administration.

Ten hours released is not automatically ten hours of cash savings. The business might use that capacity for other work without reducing payroll or expenses. Keep that distinction visible when justifying automation.

The calculator below estimates first-year ROI and simple payback from values you supply. It runs in your browser and does not send your inputs to a service. It is a planning aid, not a forecast of what a subscription will achieve.

Start with a real baseline

Choose one repeatable workflow. Record how frequently it happens and how long the relevant work currently takes. Include the time needed to resolve errors. Do not assume every minute disappears after automation: people may still review the output and maintain the connection.

Estimate the hours that could realistically be released each month. Use a lower case as well as a base case. Assign an hourly value only if you can explain what that value represents—staff cost, an avoided external charge or useful capacity for other work.

Estimated value may represent released capacity, not cash saved. No taxes, financing, discounting or guarantee of benefit is included.

The recurring monthly input should include subscription fees, expected usage charges and ongoing administration. Setup should include one-time configuration, training and transition costs. Count each cost once. Use one currency consistently; the dollar label is an example convention, not an exchange-rate service.

Understand the formulas

Monthly estimated value equals hours released multiplied by the hourly value. Annual value is twelve times that figure. First-year cost equals setup plus twelve times recurring monthly cost.

First-year ROI is annual value minus first-year cost, divided by first-year cost, multiplied by 100. Simple payback is setup divided by monthly value minus recurring monthly cost, assuming that net benefit is positive and remains constant.

If net monthly benefit is zero or negative, the example has no positive simple payback. If first-year cost is zero, the usual percentage ROI is undefined. A positive percentage is not proof that a project should be approved; the assumptions can still be wrong.

Compare more than one scenario

Here is a hypothetical example with US$1,200 setup, US$100 recurring monthly cost and US$30 per hour assigned value. It excludes taxes, financing and any change in the assumptions.

Monthly hours released Annual value First-year cost First-year ROI Simple payback
5 US$1,800 US$2,400 -25% 24 months
10 US$3,600 US$2,400 50% 6 months
15 US$5,400 US$2,400 125% About 3.43 months

The same subscription looks very different when the released hours change. That is why a single optimistic estimate is not enough. Check whether the result still makes sense if setup takes longer, usage grows or review work reduces the time released.

Separate capacity, cash and operational risk

Time released may improve responsiveness or reduce a bottleneck even without a direct expense reduction. Describe that outcome as capacity unless you have evidence of an actual avoided charge. Do not count the same hour both as reduced payroll and additional billable revenue.

Consider what failure would cost. A low-cost workflow that sends incorrect invoices or messages clients repeatedly may have a risk the simple formula does not capture. Keep approvals and failure handling in the design.

Make the decision after a pilot

Run a bounded trial with sample or approved data. Measure the work still required, then replace estimates with observations. Record the owner, review date and conditions that would make you stop or change the workflow.

The result is a transparent business case: what was assumed, what was measured and which uncertainty remains. Pair it with the hidden-cost checklist, not with a promise that every automation pays for itself.
