Top story

France blamed Iran for the freezer attack that hit 700 fridges

10 November 2026France

Your supermarket freezer is not supposed to be a target in a fight between countries. In France, it became one.

On the night of 11 and 12 October, attackers hit 700 refrigeration systems in France. Supermarkets took the worst of it, along with a slaughterhouse, food processing plants, and hydro facilities. At least 15 fires broke out, food was damaged, and the attack had psychological impacts on the population. The French authorities described it as an unprecedented attack in terms of its scale and level of aggression.

On 9 November, France blamed Iran. It was the first time the French government had directly accused Tehran of a cyberattack, and it named those it holds responsible. France says the cyber command of the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps, known as IRGC-CEC, ordered the attack, and its Shahid Kaveh unit carried it out. The Paris prosecutor has requested international arrest warrants for three people. Hamid Reza Lashgarian, 65, heads IRGC-CEC and is a commander in the Al-Quds Force. He has been under US anti-terrorism sanctions since February 2024. Reza Salarvand, 35, is responsible for target selection and overseeing cyber intrusion campaigns. Mahdi Lashgarian, 37, is believed to play a leading role in Shahid Kaveh. Investigators pointed to a distinctive technical modus operandi, traces of Persian in the computer code, and IP addresses located in Iran. On 10 November, the Iranian ambassador in Paris was summoned.

This was not the first time the group had been noticed. It is also known as CyberAv3ngers, and it launched its first operations in November 2023. That month it took control of some equipment used in water management in the US and displayed a message of support for Gaza on device screens, without causing significant damage. Washington has offered a 10 million dollar reward for information about the group. It carried out attacks on refrigeration systems in Israel in March and April. The attack on French supermarkets is by far its most successful operation to date. The motive is still unclear. French cybersecurity firm Sekoia suggested one possible explanation: the recent deployment of French military assets to protect the Saudi oil terminal at Yanbu from attacks by pro-Iranian Houthi forces.

It did not stop with France. A second wave hit supermarkets in Belgium, the Netherlands, Luxembourg, Sweden, Estonia, and Ireland overnight into 2 November. The attackers forced fridges into defrost mode, and shops had to throw the food away. Danfoss said its controllers were involved, and France's CERT has also named Copeland and Dixell controllers. Then, on 5 November, five US agencies, CISA, the FBI, HHS, USDA, and DOT, warned that attackers are going after industrial refrigeration and cold chain systems all over the world. There is one encouraging sign. Censys counted a 53 percent drop in internet-exposed Danfoss and Copeland controllers since the October waves. Taking controllers offline is working.

Why it matters to you: this attack shows that everyday equipment can become a target. A fridge controller left open to the internet is a door into a business, and when it is forced into heating mode, food spoils and fires can start. It affects shoppers, shop owners, and anyone who depends on cold food staying cold.

What to do now

  • If you run a shop or a food business, ask your supplier a simple question. Are your controllers updated, and are they kept offline?
  • Danfoss advises disconnecting AK-SC and AK-SM controllers from the internet. A factory reset alone does not stop repeat attacks.
  • Find every internet-connected refrigeration and building controller in your business, including ones managed by suppliers, and change any default passwords.
  • If you are a shopper and a freezer feels warm, tell the staff and do not buy food that has thawed.

SonicWall / VPN security

SonicWall VPN flaw lets attackers skip multi-factor authentication

6 November 2026CVE-2026-19868

Your VPN asked for two proofs of identity. This flaw let an attacker skip the second one entirely.

A flaw in SonicWall firewalls, CVE-2026-19868, affects the SSL VPN service. Where the VPN uses SAML sign in, valid credentials for another login method can bypass the SAML controls and the multi-factor authentication they enforce. The second factor was not broken. It was simply never asked, because the attacker arrived by a different route. That is the pattern of this whole week: the strong front door held, and the attacker walked around it.

CISA added the flaw to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities list on 6 November, with a federal deadline of 20 November. The Canadian Cyber Centre has confirmed exploitation. There is no workaround. The fix is SonicOS 7.3.4-7013 for Gen7 and 8.2.3-8015 for Gen8.

Why it matters to you: a VPN is the front door to a company network, and multi-factor authentication is the lock most teams trust. If an alternate login route skips that lock, the protection you think you have is not the protection you actually have. Stolen credentials are all an attacker needs to start.

What to do now

  • If you run a SonicWall VPN, patch now to SonicOS 7.3.4-7013 for Gen7 or 8.2.3-8015 for Gen8. Do not wait for the 20 November deadline.
  • Audit every alternate authentication route on your remote access systems, and switch off login methods you do not use.
  • Review who logged in, and how, since the flaw was disclosed, and rotate or review credentials that look wrong.
  • If you use a company VPN, ask your IT team whether your SonicWall is patched. It is a fair question, and it takes a minute to ask.

WordPress / Website security

WordPress flaw lets attackers run code during updates

3 November 2026CVE-2026-119977

The most dangerous moment for a WordPress site is not when it is running. It is when it is updating.

A race condition in the way WordPress applies core updates, CVE-2026-119977, lets an attacker with no login at all run code during the upgrade window. The attacker reaches a predictable staging folder while the site is updating. It affects WordPress 4.7 to 7.1.3. WordPress fixed the flaw on 28 October, and CISA added it to its catalogue on 3 November, with a federal deadline of 6 November.

Attackers moved fast. Wordfence counted more than 18,500 probes of the staging paths in the first 21 hours. Those were attempts, not compromises, but Wordfence reports it is blocking thousands of attempts a day. The lesson is blunt. Twice in one week, the maintenance path was the attack path, and a patch that exists is not the same as a patch that is installed.

Why it matters to you: if your site, or a site you pay someone to run, uses WordPress, the risk window is the update itself. An attacker needs no password and no account. They only need to arrive at the right second, while files are landing in a folder whose address they can guess.

What to do now

  • Update to WordPress 7.1.4, or the fixed release for your branch, for example 7.0.8, 6.9.11, or 6.8.12.
  • Block public web access to the wp-content/upgrade folder.
  • Do not leave upgrades half done. Finish the update, then check the site still works.
  • If someone else manages your site, ask them today which WordPress version you are on.

Amadeus / Travel bookings

Amadeus breach widens as Singapore Airlines reports 46,000 records affected

4 November 2026Singapore Airlines

You did not lose money in this breach. That is exactly why the next email about your booking is dangerous.

Singapore Airlines said on 4 November that about 46,000 booking records may have been affected through reservation provider Amadeus. It joins Cathay Pacific, Japan Airlines, ANA, Malaysia Airlines, and Asiana in the same incident. The counts mix bookings and customers, so they are not additive. Amadeus says no payment data was taken and bookings were not modified. That is the good news.

The awkward part is what booking details alone can do. A name, a flight number, and a travel date are enough to write an email that looks exactly like it came from your airline. It can ask you to confirm a change, re-enter payment details, or click a link to view your booking. Nothing about it will look wrong, because the details in it are real.

Why it matters to you: this is a third-party breach, which means you did nothing wrong and could not have prevented it. Your data was held by a provider your airline uses, not by you. The risk now is not the breach itself. It is the convincing scam that follows it.

What to do now

  • Treat unexpected emails about your booking with care, even if they use your real flight details.
  • Do not click links in a booking email. Go to the airline website or app yourself and check there.
  • Never share payment details by email or through a link in a message.
  • If you flew with one of these airlines, stay alert for phishing that uses your trip details over the next few weeks.

Iris Plaza / Online shopping

Iris Plaza breach may have exposed up to 7.7 million people

8 November 2026Iris Plaza

7.7 million people. That is the number at the centre of Japan's biggest shopping breach this month, and passwords were in the mix.

Unauthorised access to an external cloud service used by the Iris Plaza store may have exposed up to 7.7 million people. That includes 5.8 million members and former members, including passwords, and 1.9 million non-members. The figure is a potential exposure under investigation, not a confirmed count of stolen records, and no misuse had been reported as of 5 November. Still, 7.7 million is a very big number, and passwords were in the mix.

Iris Plaza was not alone. In the same week, the Arara Message breach grew to about 77 million email addresses taken or possibly taken, and attackers used the service itself to send up to 7.13 million phishing emails. Arara was the fifth Japanese email delivery service breached in a single month. On 6 November, the Japanese government compiled an emergency countermeasures package, and the National Police Agency opened a nationwide joint investigation. When the same layer fails five times in a month, it becomes a property of the layer, not a run of bad luck.

Why it matters to you: a password that leaks in one breach is tried everywhere else. If you used the same password for Iris Plaza and another account, that other account is now at risk too, even though it was never breached.

What to do now

  • If you have an Iris Plaza account, change your password now, and do not reuse it anywhere else.
  • Change the same password on any other account where you used it.
  • Turn on two-factor authentication where it is offered, especially on email and shopping accounts.
  • Watch for phishing emails that use your shopping details, and do not click links in unexpected messages.

AI news

OpenAI / Images

ChatGPT Images 2.6 turns your pictures into GIFs, on every plan

4 November 2026OpenAI

Good news if you like making things with AI. OpenAI launched Images 2.6 on 4 November, and the best part is the price. It is on every subscription tier, not only the expensive plan. So you can try it without upgrading. It is available globally in ChatGPT, on desktop, mobile, and web.

Three things changed that you will notice. First, speed. OpenAI says images generate 50 percent faster. Second, GIFs. You can animate a mascot, a product shot, or a simple sketch into a loop. Third, consistency. Characters, products, and brand details are meant to stay put as you edit, instead of your logo slowly melting with every revision. If you have a developer, the API version also exports SVG files, which you can drop straight onto a website or into print.

Why it matters to you: a small business can now make a simple animated product shot without paying for a higher plan. That is a free animated image for your next email or product page, made from a photo you already own.

What to do now

  • Take one product photo you own and ask for a short loop. Try this: turn this product photo into a smooth looping GIF. Keep the product, label, and colours exactly as they are. Only the background light should move.
  • Check the label at full size before you publish. AI loves to improve small print, and a misspelt ingredient is not a fun conversation with a customer.
  • Use photos you own or have the rights to, and keep the result honest. Do not present an AI image as a real photo of your product.

Source: Lilach Bullock

Google / Video

Gemini Omni 1.2 Flash makes video with sound, and costs less

5 November 2026Google DeepMind

Short video just got cheaper, and you can now edit it by talking to it.

Google DeepMind published the model card for Gemini Omni 1.2 Flash on 5 November, and the model is generally available. It makes video, with audio, from text, images, audio, or video. Clips run up to 15 seconds instead of 10, and Google says pricing is 30 percent below the previous version. In plain words, you type or show it something, and you get a short video back, for less money than before.

The part that matters most for small teams is the editing. You tell it what to change and it changes it, rather than you dragging clips around a timeline at midnight. Google lists the Gemini app, YouTube, Google Flow, AI Studio, and Google Workspace among the places it is distributed, but it does not promise a date for your account, so check before you plan a campaign around it. Two details are worth knowing. Google says the videos carry SynthID watermarks, so AI content can be identified. And the model can change what people say in a video, but Google is restricting that for now.

Why it matters to you: a video workflow you shelved six months ago because it cost too much is worth another look. Short social clips are now cheaper to make, and the editing happens in plain words instead of specialist software.

What to do now

  • Try one short clip for social media, and price it against what a freelancer or a shoot would cost you.
  • Check every frame for wrong labels or odd hands before you use a clip.
  • Never edit a customer, a client, or a member of staff to say something they did not say. Not even as a joke.
  • Check that Gemini Omni 1.2 Flash is available in your account before you promise a video to a client.

Google DeepMind / Encryption

DeepMind found two weaknesses in old 1024-bit RSA encryption

6 November 2026Google DeepMind

No, your encryption is not broken. But AI just did a job that used to need a career of intuition, and that changes the clock.

Google DeepMind said on 6 November that its AI agents, supervised by humans and working with academic cryptographers, found two previously unknown weaknesses in 1024-bit RSA encryption. RSA is the maths behind a lot of older digital signatures and key exchange, and the number is the key length. 1024-bit keys left serious use years ago. The team included Nadia Heninger of UC San Diego and Emmanuel Thome of Inria, and the work was announced by Pushmeet Kohli, DeepMind's VP of science.

The first weakness concerns padding, the extra structure wrapped around a message before signing to make forgery harder. Once an attacker controls about half the signed block, the team says, padding buys almost nothing. Where the attacker controls only a short fingerprint, the agents produced an algorithm DeepMind estimates is around 1,600 times faster than the best published method. The second is batch factoring. When many keys share a bit length, one expensive precomputation can be spread across all of them, cutting the average cost per additional key by roughly 30 times.

Now the honest part. Both multipliers are theoretical rather than measured end to end. The part that ran was a sandboxed reproduction against a 1024-bit key an academic team had already broken, published on the IACR ePrint archive in September. There, DeepMind reports its agents used 3.5 times less CPU time than factoring the key outright. No production system still relying on 1024-bit RSA has been named, and DeepMind says modern encryption is untouched. 2048-bit keys are unaffected.

Why it matters to you: encryption did not fall over. What moved is that the slow, intuition-heavy part of cryptanalysis is now something a machine can take a real run at, under supervision, in weeks. That shortens the honest answer to how long any given assumption keeps holding. The job this week is an inventory, not a panic.

What to do now

  • Find out whether anything you run still signs or exchanges keys with a 1024-bit certificate. It usually turns up in old internal services and embedded hardware, not in anything public.
  • Retire any 1024-bit keys you find, and replace them with modern key sizes.
  • Do not rush to change modern encryption. This finding does not break the encryption used today.
  • If a vendor tells you a system still needs 1024-bit RSA, ask them for their plan to move off it.
