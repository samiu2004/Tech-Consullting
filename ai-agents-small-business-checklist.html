AI Agents for Small Businesses: Test Permissions, Logs and Failure Handling

Evaluate what an AI service may change, who approves it and how mistakes are contained.

By TechConsulting EditorialPublished 15 November 20263 min read

Before asking what an agent can do, ask what it can change without approval. A convincing demonstration of a routine task says little about how a service handles an unusual request, a failed connection or a mistaken interpretation.

This guide evaluates an operational capability. It does not claim that agents are the fastest-growing software category or that adopting one produces a particular business result. A product label is not enough evidence for either claim.

Distinguish a fixed workflow from delegated judgment

For buying purposes, a useful distinction is whether you define a fixed sequence or allow a system to choose actions within a permitted scope. Products can combine both approaches, and vendors use “agent” differently. Ask for a concrete explanation of the proposed task rather than relying on the label.

Start with a bounded job. Preparing a draft response from approved material is a different responsibility from changing a customer's account or issuing money. The permission and review model should reflect the action, not the excitement of the demonstration.

Ask what the service can access

List connected systems, readable data and writable actions. Identify the identity used for each connection and whether permissions can be limited to the intended task. Avoid granting broad account access merely because it makes a pilot easier.

Ask what happens when access expires, a staff member leaves or the service receives conflicting instructions. Keep an owner responsible for reviewing connected permissions and disabling the capability if it behaves unexpectedly.

Define approval and escalation

Question Evidence to request
What can it change? A list of actions and permission scope
Which actions need approval? A demonstrated approval gate
What happens when it is uncertain? A real escalation or refusal path
How are mistakes investigated? Relevant execution history and access controls
How do we stop it? Documented disable and recovery procedure
What creates a charge? Current billing definitions and allowances

Not every log contains the same information. Ask what is recorded, which plan includes it, who can inspect it and how long it remains available. Do not assume the word “audit” guarantees a complete explanation of every decision.

Test the exceptions, not just the demonstration

Use approved sample data. Include an ordinary request, an ambiguous request, a missing record, an unavailable connected system and an instruction outside the intended scope. Record the expected behavior before running the trial.

The appropriate result might be a draft, a request for clarification or a handoff to a person. A confident answer is not a successful result if it is wrong or takes an unauthorized action. Inspect both the user-facing response and the system changes.

Check cost independently from capability

Some services combine seat charges with usage or outcome charges. For example, Intercom's official material defines billable Fin outcomes. Read the provider's exact definition rather than assuming “outcome” means a successful business result as your team would measure it.

Use the pricing-model guide to inspect minimums, caps and charging events. A capability that performs well in a short pilot may still have a different cost profile at routine volume.

Make a limited adoption decision

Approve a scope, owner and review date. Keep a way to stop the automation and continue important work manually. Expand only after the relevant tests, permissions and operating costs are understood.

For a straightforward trigger-and-action task, a fixed workflow may be easier to specify and verify. Do not choose an agent merely because the existing product now offers one. The decision should follow the work, the consequences of an error and the evidence from a bounded evaluation.
