DeepMind found two weaknesses in old 1024-bit RSA encryption

No, your encryption is not broken. But AI just did a job that used to need a career of intuition, and that changes the clock.

By TechConsulting Editorial Published 6 November 2026 Sources checked 13 November 2026 3 min read

No, your encryption is not broken. But AI just did a job that used to need a career of intuition, and that changes the clock.

Google DeepMind said on 6 November that its AI agents, supervised by humans and working with academic cryptographers, found two previously unknown weaknesses in 1024-bit RSA encryption. RSA is the maths behind a lot of older digital signatures and key exchange, and the number is the key length. 1024-bit keys left serious use years ago. The team included Nadia Heninger of UC San Diego and Emmanuel Thome of Inria, and the work was announced by Pushmeet Kohli, DeepMind's VP of science.

The first weakness concerns padding, the extra structure wrapped around a message before signing to make forgery harder. Once an attacker controls about half the signed block, the team says, padding buys almost nothing. Where the attacker controls only a short fingerprint, the agents produced an algorithm DeepMind estimates is around 1,600 times faster than the best published method. The second is batch factoring. When many keys share a bit length, one expensive precomputation can be spread across all of them, cutting the average cost per additional key by roughly 30 times.

Now the honest part. Both multipliers are theoretical rather than measured end to end. The part that ran was a sandboxed reproduction against a 1024-bit key an academic team had already broken, published on the IACR ePrint archive in September. There, DeepMind reports its agents used 3.5 times less CPU time than factoring the key outright. No production system still relying on 1024-bit RSA has been named, and DeepMind says modern encryption is untouched. 2048-bit keys are unaffected.

Why it matters to you: encryption did not fall over. What moved is that the slow, intuition-heavy part of cryptanalysis is now something a machine can take a real run at, under supervision, in weeks. That shortens the honest answer to how long any given assumption keeps holding. The job this week is an inventory, not a panic.

What to do now

  • Find out whether anything you run still signs or exchanges keys with a 1024-bit certificate. It usually turns up in old internal services and embedded hardware, not in anything public.
  • Retire any 1024-bit keys you find, and replace them with modern key sizes.
  • Do not rush to change modern encryption. This finding does not break the encryption used today.
  • If a vendor tells you a system still needs 1024-bit RSA, ask them for their plan to move off it.
